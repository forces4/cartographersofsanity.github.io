Copilot said
Great question — you’re touching on the subtle physics of how superconducting magnets are actually operated in large machines like ITER or particle accelerators. Let’s break it down clearly:

⚡ How superconducting magnets maintain their field
Persistent current mode (closed loop):

In principle, a superconducting coil can be energized once, then disconnected from the external power supply.

Because superconductors have zero DC resistance, the current can circulate indefinitely, maintaining the magnetic field without further input.

This is often used in smaller systems, like superconducting magnets in MRI machines or laboratory setups, where a “persistent switch” (a small piece of superconductor that can be heated to allow current injection, then cooled to lock the loop) is employed.

Driven mode (constant external current):

In very large systems like ITER or particle accelerators, magnets are usually kept connected to a power supply.

The reason is practical: these magnets require precise control of current and field strength, ramping up and down during operation. Disconnecting them would make it difficult to adjust or stabilize the field.

ITER’s magnets, for example, are powered continuously by massive external current leads, because they need dynamic control during plasma confinement【edge_current_page_context】.

🔋 Do superconductors lose energy?
Even though superconductors eliminate resistive losses, they are not perfectly lossless in real-world applications:

Flux creep and flux flow:

In type-II superconductors (like Nb₃Sn and Nb–Ti used in ITER), magnetic vortices penetrate the material. These vortices can slowly move (“creep”) under current and field stress, causing tiny energy dissipation.

AC losses:

If the current or magnetic field is changing (ramping up/down), superconductors experience hysteresis and eddy-current-like losses.

Joint and connection losses:

While the wire itself is superconducting, connections between segments may have finite resistance, leading to small but non-negligible losses.

Cryogenic load:

The biggest “energy loss” is indirect: keeping the magnets cold. Cryogenic refrigeration to maintain liquid helium or supercooled environments consumes significant power.

🧩 Putting it together
Small-scale magnets (MRI, lab experiments): Often run in persistent mode — current flows in a closed superconducting loop, disconnected from power, with fields stable for months or years.

Large-scale magnets (ITER, LHC, particle accelerators): Typically run in driven mode — connected to external power supplies for control, stability, and safety.

Energy losses: Not from resistance in the wire itself, but from flux motion, AC cycling, imperfect joints, and the huge cryogenic systems needed to keep everything superconducting.

👉 So: yes, superconductors can maintain current without external power, but in practice, the giants like ITER and accelerators keep them powered continuously for control. The “losses” are more about magnetic vortex dynamics and refrigeration than ohmic resistance.

⚛️ Superconductivity basics
Superconductivity arises when electrons form Cooper pairs — bound states mediated by lattice vibrations (phonons) or other interactions.

The “electron density patterns” you mention are indeed central: it’s the collective quantum state of electrons that matters, not the identity of the atoms per se.

However, the atoms provide the scaffolding — their lattice vibrations, spacing, and electronic orbitals create the conditions for pairing.

🧩 Synthetic routes to mimic superconductivity
Researchers are actively exploring ways to engineer superconducting-like states without relying on unstable materials:

Artificial lattices (STM corrals, moiré materials):

By arranging atoms with STM tips or stacking 2D materials at specific angles (like twisted bilayer graphene), scientists can create electron density patterns that mimic superconducting states.

Example: “magic-angle graphene” shows superconductivity at relatively high temperatures, purely from engineered electron interference patterns.

Cold atom quantum simulators:

Ultracold atoms in optical lattices can be tuned to reproduce the Hubbard model — the key theoretical framework for high-temperature superconductivity.

These systems don’t superconduct in the electrical sense (no current flows), but they simulate the electron pairing physics, helping us design real materials.

Metamaterials and photonic analogues:

Synthetic structures can mimic the band structures and density of states that favor superconductivity.

While photons don’t form Cooper pairs, these analogues can guide us toward engineered electron systems.

Hydrides under pressure:

Instead of pure metallic hydrogen, researchers have stabilized hydrogen-rich compounds (like lanthanum hydride, sulfur hydride) that superconduct at near-room temperature — but only under extreme pressures.

The dream is to mimic those electron density conditions in a stable synthetic lattice at ambient pressure.

🔮 The frontier idea
What you’re suggesting — a synthetic state that mimics superconductivity without the unstable material — is exactly the motivation behind:

Designer quantum materials (graphene stacks, oxide interfaces).

Quantum simulators (cold atoms, STM arrays).

Computational material design (using AI to predict stable compounds with the right electron density patterns).

The challenge is that superconductivity isn’t just electron density — it’s also the dynamic coupling to lattice vibrations or other mediators. So while we can mimic the patterns, achieving a true, room-temperature, stable superconducting current requires both the electron correlations and a medium that supports them.

🧩 Summary
Yes, superconductivity is about electron patterns, not the atoms themselves.

Synthetic systems (STM arrays, moiré materials, cold atom simulators) can mimic the physics of superconductivity.

The holy grail is to translate those synthetic mimics into real, ambient-pressure materials that carry current without resistance.

We’re not there yet, but the path you’re describing is exactly where much of the cutting-edge research is heading.
