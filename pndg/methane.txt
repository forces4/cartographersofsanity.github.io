Methane clathrates—also called “fire ice”—exist in a fragile equilibrium of cold temperatures and high pressure on the ocean floor. As oceans warm, especially in the Arctic and continental slope regions, this balance begins to falter.

🔥 Ignition and Thermobaric Imagery: Is That Plausible?
The image of methane igniting and turning animals inside out like a thermobaric weapon is viscerally powerful—but not scientifically likely in the literal sense.

Methane ignition underwater is extremely rare due to lack of oxygen and pressure conditions.

Atmospheric ignition would require concentrated methane clouds and a spark—possible near industrial leaks, but not on a planetary scale.

The real danger is thermal and chemical, not explosive: methane traps heat, destabilizes climate systems, and causes mass mortality through heatwaves, crop failure, and ecosystem collapse.

⏳ Timeline from Pulse to Peril
If the thermal pulse reaches vulnerable clathrates:

Initial destabilization: Within years to decades, methane begins bubbling up—first underwater, then into the atmosphere.

Amplified warming: Within 10–30 years, feedback loops accelerate. Arctic ice vanishes, permafrost thaws, and climate zones shift.

Mass human mortality: If unchecked, this could begin within 30–50 years, driven by heat stress, famine, displacement, and conflict—not explosions, but slow unraveling.

🌡️ PETM: A Hot Pulse in Earth’s History
The Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM) occurred about 56 million years ago. It was one of the most abrupt and intense warming events in Earth’s history:

Global temperatures rose by 5–8°C in less than 20,000 years

Ocean acidification and anoxia spread rapidly

Massive carbon release—likely from methane clathrates, volcanism, and permafrost melt—drove the warming
Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution
+1

It wasn’t the hottest period ever (that title likely belongs to the Mesozoic hothouse), but PETM was uniquely rapid and chaotic.

🦎 Did Only Reptiles Survive?
Not quite. During the PETM:

Mammals diversified, especially small, heat-tolerant species

Reptiles thrived in tropical zones, but didn’t dominate globally

Marine life suffered, especially deep-sea organisms and coral ecosystems

No mass extinction, but major turnover in species and ecosystems

It was more of a biological reshuffling than a full collapse—though some lineages never recovered.
