The Two Who Live Inside Us
They were born together,
but they did not speak the same tongue.

One arrived fluent in music,
in the tilt of a face,
in the way a shadow leans when someone is hurting.
It felt the world in whole constellations,
never in single stars.
It could not form a sentence,
but it could read a room like scripture.

The other arrived with grammar in its bones,
a builder of ladders and labels,
a cartographer of cause and effect.
It could speak, argue, name, define.
But it could not always feel  
the thing it was describing.

Nature — that old, ruthless engineer —
looked at these two mismatched creatures
and said,
Yes.
Fuse them.
Let them figure it out.

And so they did.

They learned to pass notes under the table.
They learned to trade strengths like tools.
They learned that neither could survive alone.

One said:
I will give you intuition,
the shape of the forest before the first tree is seen.

The other said:
I will give you articulation,
the bridge from sensation to meaning.

Together they became a single mind
that could both weep at a melody
and calculate the arc of a falling stone.
A mind that could love and reason,
dream and plan,
feel and speak.

And here is the quiet truth:

Human society is built the same way.

We are thrown together —
the empath and the analyst,
the dreamer and the doer,
the poet and the engineer —
not because we match,
but because we don’t.

One person sees the world in stories.
Another sees it in systems.
One feels the wound.
Another knows how to stitch it.
One cries out.
Another answers.

We are a species of mismatched hemispheres
forced to share a skull the size of a planet.

And when we work,
when we listen,
when we let our differences braid instead of break,
something miraculous happens:

A civilization emerges
from the same principle
that lets a human mind exist at all.

Two wildly different beings
learning to be one.
